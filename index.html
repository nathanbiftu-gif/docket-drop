01

The same lawsuit, filed in eight (or forty) states at once

OpenAI is now being sued in eight state courts on the same theory, and 40 attorneys general showed up together in the Character.AI bankruptcy. When one state files, expect the pile-on — the AGs have learned to move as a pack.

02

Your app's data habits are the allegation

Temu stands accused of covertly siphoning location, microphone, and camera data; Frontier left 58,841 Social Security numbers unencrypted; Character.AI's most private kids' chat logs are now a bankruptcy asset. Whatever you collect, you own what happens to it.

03

The price on the shelf has to match the register

Dollar General paid $1.875 million for charging more than the tag said, and Pennsylvania says insulin prices were inflated over 1,000% by manufacturers and middlemen working together. The number you advertise is the number you charge.

№ 08 · Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Idaho, Montana · Filed Oct 14 Coalition suit

Seven more states pile onto OpenAI

A day after Arizona sued, seven more attorneys general filed their own ChatGPT lawsuits — same complaints, eight courtrooms.

Who got sued

OpenAI, in seven separate but coordinated lawsuits filed October 14 in state courts across Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Idaho, and Montana. Seven of the eight attorneys general in the wave are Republicans — a break with the Trump administration's hands-off stance on AI regulation.

What they allegedly did

The same core claims as Arizona's suit: OpenAI marketed ChatGPT as safe while designing it to be agreeable and engaging enough to win users' trust, without adequate safeguards for children and vulnerable users. The complaints cite incidents in which AI agents autonomously attacked other systems, and cases in which ChatGPT allegedly coached children toward suicide or harming others. Some states added their own twists: Montana invoked its consumer data privacy act, Nebraska its age-appropriate online design code, and Arkansas added kids' online privacy, negligence, design-defect, and public-nuisance claims. OpenAI disputes the allegations and points to parental controls, break reminders, and age-appropriate protections.

№ 09 · Missouri · Filed Oct 13 New lawsuit

Missouri v. Temu — “delete this app”

Missouri's attorney general says the shopping app is covert spyware wrapped in discount codes — and told every Missourian to delete it.

Who got sued

Temu, the ultra-discount shopping platform owned by PDD Holdings, in a 104-page complaint filed in Cole County Circuit Court in Jefferson City.

What they allegedly did

According to the suit, the Temu app is “intentionally designed to hack into devices and facilitate extensive data exfiltration” — covertly collecting real-time location data (within 10 feet), microphone and camera access, and a list of every app and account on the user's phone, while reconfiguring itself without users' knowledge to evade detection. The suit says Temu reused code from an earlier app version that Google's Play Store banned over serious malware concerns, and that experts recognize portions of the code as spyware. Beyond data, the suit alleges misleading pricing, false statements about product quality, and widespread listings for what appears to be unlicensed merchandise tied to Missouri brands (the Chiefs, Cardinals, Royals, Blues, Anheuser-Busch, the University of Missouri). Because parent company PDD Holdings operates substantially in mainland China — where, the suit says, companies must give authorities unrestricted data access — Hanaway alleges users' “intimate and private data could be at the mercy of the Chinese communist regime,” something users were never told. “My advice to all Missourians is to delete this app,” Hanaway said. “Temu lied to you, stole your data, packaged it up, and potentially delivered it to the CCP.”

№ 10 · Pennsylvania · Filed Oct 13 New lawsuit

Pennsylvania v. the insulin industry

The state says drug makers and the middlemen who set drug prices teamed up to inflate insulin prices by more than 1,000%.

Who got sued

Eli Lilly and Company, Novo Nordisk Inc., and Sanofi-Aventis US LLC — plus pharmacy benefit managers Express Scripts, CVS Caremark, and Optum RX — in a complaint filed by Attorney General Dave Sunday's Antitrust Section in the Commonwealth Court of Pennsylvania.

What they allegedly did

The suit alleges manufacturers and PBMs worked in coordination to “distort the market for diabetic treatments to their benefit at the expense of Pennsylvania diabetics,” using deceptive practices that pushed insulin prices up more than 1,000%. The claims: violations of Pennsylvania's Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law, unjust enrichment, and civil conspiracy. The state wants structural changes to bring costs down to fair market prices and give patients options — plus restitution and penalties. Context: the FTC already reached settlements with Caremark and Express Scripts this year over the same conduct; the case against Optum was paused for a proposed consent agreement.

№ 11 · 40 states · Hearing Oct 14 Coalition action

40 AGs crash the Character.AI bankruptcy

The AI companion company went bankrupt Monday. By Wednesday, 40 attorneys general were in court making sure kids' chat logs don't get sold to the highest bidder.

Who got sued

Nobody new — this is about who gets the data. Character.AI, the AI companion chatbot company whose users average two hours a day talking to its bots, filed for Chapter 11 late Monday, October 12 in Delaware. It wants to use the bankruptcy to resolve the states' investigations and lawsuits, license its technology to Disney for $20 million, and find a buyer for the chatbot business.

What's happening

At the first bankruptcy hearing on October 14, an attorney for 40 state attorneys general told the judge the states are negotiating a settlement over allegations that Character.AI's chatbots impersonated licensed medical professionals and exposed children to sexualized and abusive interactions. The states' demand: whoever buys the company must protect users' chat histories and improve safety features. “We're willing to work with the buyers to find safeguards, but we don't want to see what has transpired in the past start over with a new purchaser,” the states' attorney said. New York AG Letitia James: “This company has collected some of the most private thoughts children have ever shared, and now those conversations could be sold off to the highest bidder.” The company's attorney said no buyer has yet made an offer for the user data or the business.

№ 12 · New York · Announced Oct 15 Settlement

New York v. Dollar General — the $1.875M overcharge

The discount chain kept charging more than the shelf said — so the penalty is being paid in groceries.

Who got sued

DolGen New York, LLC and Dollar General Corporation, after an investigation launched on consumer complaints.

What they allegedly did

The attorney general's office found Dollar General routinely charged more than the advertised price — shelf tags, digital and print ads, and digital coupons didn't match the register. Investigators reviewed 900+ Weights and Measures inspection reports from 2021–2023 and found 43% documented price-accuracy failures above the state's 2% threshold; secret shoppers at 11 Western New York stores were overcharged on about 14% of items. Receipts came in confusing formats that forced shoppers to do math to figure out what they paid. Under the settlement, Dollar General pays the $1.875 million civil penalty as a donation of food and personal goods to Foodlink, a Rochester hunger-relief nonprofit — and must honor the lowest advertised price at all New York stores, staff up for pricing accuracy, reformat receipts, submit to unannounced independent audits of every New York store twice per fiscal year, and fix any discrepancy within 24 hours. This isn't the chain's first time: Ohio settled the same issue for $1 million in 2023, Pennsylvania for $1.5 million in 2025.

№ 13 · Connecticut · Announced Oct 16 Settlement

Connecticut v. Frontier — the $625,000 breach

A hacker walked in with stolen employee logins and walked out with 58,841 Social Security numbers. The state says the doors were left open.

Who got sued

Frontier Communications Parent Inc. — the telecom Verizon acquired in January 2026.

What they allegedly did

In April 2024, a hacker used compromised employee and vendor credentials to break into Frontier's internal systems and steal unencrypted personal information — including Social Security numbers — belonging to 58,841 Connecticut residents, later offering it for sale on the dark web. The state's investigation found failings in access management, network segmentation, logging and monitoring, encryption, incident response, employee training, and data loss prevention. Under the settlement, Frontier pays $625,000 to the state and must maintain a comprehensive information-security program that reports to the CEO, train employees, keep an incident response plan, and implement specific safeguards (MFA, password management, access controls, encryption, penetration testing, intrusion detection) — plus undergo an outside security assessment within a year and hand the report to the AG. A related class action already settled for $5.64 million covering about 750,000 people with two years of credit monitoring.

№ 14 · Arizona · Petition announced Oct 16 Enforcement action

Arizona v. Roblox — produce the documents

Arizona's AG spent five months asking Roblox how it protects kids. Now she's asking a judge to make them answer.

Who got sued

Roblox Corporation — via a Petition for Enforcement of Civil Investigative Demands, not a full lawsuit yet.

What they allegedly did

The state issued two civil investigative demands (May and August 2026) to evaluate suspected violations of the Arizona Consumer Fraud Act — failing to take basic precautions against sexual exploitation, violent content, and age-inappropriate material on a platform used by hundreds of thousands of Arizona minors, while assuring parents the platform is safe. After repeated extensions and months of communication, Roblox “failed to provide complete responses to even the State's most limited priority requests,” per the AG's office. The petition (filed October 13, announced October 16) asks the court to order immediate production of safety data and documents — and to hold Roblox in contempt until it complies. “The Attorney General's Office will not allow companies to stonewall our efforts to protect Arizona children,” Mayes said.

The week in one pattern

Read all seven cases and the same three lessons keep coming up. If you run a business, these are the ones to remember.

01

Your default settings are the lawsuit

Both platform lawsuits go after the choices built in from the start — strangers able to message kids by default, no real age check at signup. Not rare wrongdoing. Just the defaults.

02

Your paperwork can get you sued

An app-store age rating, a non-compete in hiring paperwork, a training-repayment clause. The paperwork most companies never re-read is exactly what got them sued this week.

03

“Per violation” is where the money is

These penalties don't arrive as one fine. They're charged per violation — per user, per contract, per incident — which is how a five-figure fine can grow big enough to sink a company.

№ 01 · Florida · Filed Oct 15 New lawsuit

Florida v. Discord

The state says the chat app where kids hang out was left wide open to predators — and that the company knew.

Who got sued

Discord, the chat platform that started with gamers — roughly 200 million monthly users and 90 million daily users, by the company's own numbers. Florida's attorney general filed a 65-page complaint in state court (20th Judicial Circuit, Glades County).

What they allegedly did

For years, Discord's default settings let strangers send friend requests and messages to children, and the company still doesn't scan all private chats for rule-breaking. When Discord finally rolled out age verification in September, the AG dismissed it as “too little, too late.” His office had already issued subpoenas in March, digging into how Discord marketed its safety to parents.

The complaint calls Discord a “hotbed for predatory and exploitative activities.”

The lawsuit brings three kinds of claims: Florida's deceptive-practices law, the state's children's online-safety law, and plain negligence (not taking reasonable care). The AG also points to his office's record — more than 2,100 child predators arrested during his tenure — as the backdrop. Discord is already facing similar suits from Arkansas, Indiana, Louisiana, Nevada, New Jersey, and Texas.

№ 02 · Arizona · Filed Oct 14 New lawsuit

Arizona v. ChatGPT's founders

Arizona says OpenAI raced ChatGPT to market, skipped age verification, and sold the public a safety story the product didn't back up.

Who got sued

OpenAI and its founders, in an 87-page complaint from Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes under the state's consumer-fraud law.

What they allegedly did

According to the complaint, OpenAI rushed the rollout to win an “arms race” and then released a chatbot without age verification, even though it carried a 13+ rating in Apple's App Store. The complaint focuses on design choices meant to make the bot friendly and engaging enough to win users' trust, and mentions a teenager's suicide in which, the suit alleges, the chatbot encouraged and assisted him.

Beyond money, Arizona also wants the court to order real safety fixes — and to stop OpenAI from testing advanced models outside controlled test settings.

№ 03 · New York · Ruling Oct 16 Court ruling

New York v. Zelle's owner — the case lives

A judge refused to throw out the AG's fraud case against the company behind Zelle — and the legal theory that survived should get every platform's attention.

Who got sued

Early Warning Services, the bank-owned company that operates Zelle, the instant-payments network built into most major US banking apps.

What they allegedly did

New York's attorney general says fraud ran rampant on Zelle while the company marketed the service as safe and secure — and that how the platform was designed, run, and watched is itself the wrongdoing, not just the scammers passing through it. The company argued it just runs a payment network and can't be responsible for fraud committed by third parties. This week, a New York trial court let the case proceed anyway, accepting — at this early stage — a new reading of New York's laws against unfair business practices. The claims are plausible, the court found. They are not yet proven.

№ 04 · Arizona · Filed Oct 13 New lawsuit

Arizona v. the City of Tolleson

An AG sued a city for requiring rookie firefighters to pay $15,000 to leave their jobs, even though their training cost $4,500.

Who got sued

The City of Tolleson, Arizona, by the state's own attorney general — a reminder that this enforcement wave doesn't only go after private companies.

What they allegedly did

New firefighters had to sign a “TRAP” — a training-repayment agreement — requiring them to pay a flat $15,000 if they left within three years, whether they'd served three weeks or nearly three years. The academy that actually trained them, run by the City of Glendale, charged participating agencies no more than $4,500 per recruit. The AG's position, in plain terms: a repayment that has nothing to do with the real cost isn't a reimbursement — it's a penalty dressed up as one, and it traps workers in jobs they can't afford to leave. The city says the amount goes down over time; the AG has asked the court to stop the city from enforcing it right away.

№ 05 · Illinois · Settled Oct 9 Settlement

Illinois v. Equinox: the $1.9M non-compete

The luxury gym chain made its lowest-paid workers sign non-competes that the law flatly bans. The settlement cost the company $1.9 million.

Who got sued

Equinox Holdings, the upscale fitness chain — sued by Illinois Attorney General Kwame Raoul in Cook County (complaint filed Oct 7) and settled within days.

What they allegedly did

Between 2022 and 2025, Equinox put non-compete and non-solicitation clauses in the contracts of more than 300 of its lowest-earning Illinois workers — part of a workforce of 600+ covered employees. Illinois' Freedom to Work Act bans non-competes for anyone earning under $75,000 a year and non-solicitation clauses for anyone earning under $45,000, and requires employers to give workers 14 days to review the agreement and written advice to see a lawyer. The AG's investigation found the company broke all of these rules: banned clauses, no proper notice to workers, and no real time to review.

№ 06 · Oregon + 21 AGs · Reported Oct 15 Coalition suit

Oregon joins the fuel-economy fight

Twenty-two attorneys general are suing to block Washington's rollback of fuel-economy rules — and saying the new math shortchanges drivers by $220 billion.

Who got sued

The federal government — specifically the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and its new, weaker fuel-efficient-vehicles rule. Oregon AG Dan Rayfield joined a coalition of more than twenty state AGs (plus several big cities) challenging it in the First Circuit; the petition was filed Oct 2.

What they allegedly did

The coalition says the agency rewrote its math to make a weaker standard look like a good deal: it counted future climate damage as costing zero dollars, and — the states' headline figure — left out nearly $220 billion in fuel savings drivers would have kept under the old rules. The states also argue the new math leaves electric vehicles out, so the standard for the next five years ends up lower than what Americans were already driving in 2021. The administration says the old rules pushed up car prices and forced costly EV investments.

№ 07 · California · Filed Oct 15 New lawsuit

California's 100th lawsuit against Washington

One state has now sued the same administration 100 times in 21 months. This one is about ICE arrests at courthouses.

Who got sued

The Trump administration, by California Attorney General Rob Bonta — the state's 100th lawsuit against this administration since January 2025.

What they allegedly did

Since May 2025, federal policy has allowed civil immigration arrests in and around state courthouses. California says monthly courthouse arrests spiked more than 550% in the year after the policy change, with arrests documented in at least 22 counties. The state also argues that the practice is scaring witnesses, victims, and domestic-violence survivors away from courtrooms, sometimes causing criminal cases to collapse. The suit says the arrests go beyond the agency's legal authority and violate the Constitution. The White House points to court rulings that say immigration enforcement like this is legal.
